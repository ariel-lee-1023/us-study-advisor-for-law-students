# references/institutions.md

**Source:** John R. Thelin, *American Higher Education: Issues and Institutions* (Core Concepts in Higher Education, Routledge).
**Trigger:** load on **how U.S. universities and law schools are structured, stratified, funded, or governed — and what a given institution is optimizing for**.
**What this file is:** the institutional logic behind the behaviour a student encounters. No temperament language.

> **Vintage note.** Structure and mechanism below are durable; figures, programme names, and policy specifics are not. Aid-programme acronyms, tuition levels, and federal policy in particular change and must be verified.

## 1. Why this module exists

A student experiences admissions decisions, tuition, aid offers, and programme design as things that happen to them. They are outputs of an institution with a budget, a governance structure, and a position in a status hierarchy. **Predicting institutional behaviour requires knowing what the institution is optimizing for** — and it is usually optimizing for two things at once: prestige and revenue, which are related but not identical.

This is the module the core reaches for when running Red Hat Analysis on a school (`toolbox.md`), and it supplies the *why* behind the warnings in `llm-pathway.md` about revenue-driven programmes.

## 2. Cost, price, and net price — the distinction that governs every aid conversation

Thelin insists on separating three terms that journalists, legislators, counsellors, and families use interchangeably:

- **Cost** — what the institution spends to provide instruction, facilities, housing, dining, libraries, and administration. An institutional figure.
- **Price** — what the institution publishes as the charges to students and families: tuition, fees, room and board, books, supplies. The advertised figure, often called the *sticker price*.
- **Net price** — what a *particular* student actually pays after grants, loans, and discounts from all sources. The only figure that matters to that student.

**Consequences:**
- The published price is a list price, and comparing schools on it compares almost nothing.
- Cost and price move independently. A rising price is not evidence of rising cost, and a school can have high cost and low net price or the reverse.
- The right question to a school is never "what does it cost" but "what will *I* pay" — and net price is only knowable after an aid decision.

Two further distinctions Thelin draws:
- **Access vs. choice.** Being able to attend college at all is a different policy question from being able to attend a *particular* one. Aid systems often serve one and not the other.
- **Need-based vs. merit-based aid.** These have different institutional purposes: need-based aid serves access; merit aid is a recruiting instrument that buys desirable students, and it competes with need-based aid for the same pool.

Thelin also names the **"three Rs"** of the contemporary landscape — rankings, ratings, and rivalries — as the framework within which families now navigate.

## 3. Why costs rise: two competing theories

Both are named theories with named authors and they explain different things.

**Howard Bowen's revenue theory of cost.** Institutions pursuing excellence raise all the money they can and spend all the money they raise. Bowen's finding was dispassionate and data-based: there is always a "good use" for additional funds, because improving education is open-ended and never finished — so the thirst for resources is rarely exhausted. Institutions climbing in prestige and selectivity are especially predisposed to spend.

**Baumol and Bowen's disease theory (cost disease).** Drawing on studies of performing-arts organisations and skilled professional practices: in labour-intensive, quality-dependent enterprises, costs spread and cannot easily be cut. A string quartet cannot cut costs by dropping to three players. Meanwhile the pursuit of excellence means paying more for top talent. A university is in some ways a federation of many high-performance units — an opera programme, a surgical unit, a top-ranked department, a varsity team — and the syndrome repeats in each.

Together these produce what Thelin calls the **academic arms race**: a small circle of well-resourced institutions bid for the best applicants, faculty, and fellowships, their prestige rises, and they set a pattern and a pace that other institutions envy and imitate — including institutions that cannot afford it.

**What this predicts:** a school will not become cheaper because it becomes richer; new revenue is absorbed. Aspiring institutions spend hardest, so mid-tier schools climbing in the rankings are often where discretionary merit money is most available.

## 4. Budgets and how money moves internally

- **RCM — Responsibility Centered Management.** A budgeting model adopted by many universities from the early 1990s onward in which individual units retain the revenue they generate and bear their own costs. **This is the single most useful fact in this module for reading a graduate programme.** Under RCM, a professional school has a direct financial interest in enrollment, and a programme that generates tuition with low marginal cost is attractive to the unit regardless of its academic merit. This is the mechanism behind the "cash cow" warning in `llm-pathway.md` — not a moral failing but a budget structure.
- **Cross-subsidy.** Even without RCM, each institution has discretion to subsidise activities its trustees and president consider important. Some units are net contributors and some are net consumers, and which is which is rarely disclosed.
- **Merit vs. worth.** Thelin's distinction for programme evaluation: a programme may be genuinely excellent (merit) and still not be worth its cost to *this* institution given its mission (worth), or vice versa. It is the question a dean asks about a programme, and a useful one for a student to ask too.
- **Essential vs. peripheral activities**, and the **paradox of the budget** — that a document presenting itself as a neutral accounting is in fact the institution's statement of priorities. Reading a budget tells you what a school actually values, as distinct from its mission statement.
- **Privatisation** of public institutions: as state appropriations decline as a share of revenue, public universities behave more like private ones — including in pricing to out-of-state and international students, who become a revenue strategy.

**What this predicts:** where a student sits in the revenue structure shapes how they are treated. International students paying full price at a unit under RCM are a revenue source, and the recruitment intensity directed at them should be read accordingly.

## 5. Governance: why the institution behaves incoherently

Thelin's organisational picture, which is essential for calibrating expectations about institutional responsiveness:

- Organisational theorists describe colleges and universities as sites of **"organized anarchy"**, including the **garbage can model** of decision-making, in which administrators hold solutions and search institutional flotsam for problems those solutions fit.
- The underlying condition is that a campus houses numerous groups with multiple and often disparate goals. Thelin's borrowed image: a round soccer field, tilted, on which every player brings their own goal, ball, rules, and scoreboard.
- This does **not** mean nothing is accomplished. It means comparisons to a factory or a single-product company are simplistic, and that the campus is better read as a marketplace of activities.
- **Shared governance** distributes authority among trustees, administration, and faculty; the faculty's authority over curriculum and academic standards is real and is not the administration's to override casually.
- Bureaucracy is not the pathology it is taken for. Thelin argues an excellent bureaucracy — payroll, registrar, financial aid, grant administration — is *essential* to academic work, and its competence is noticed only in its absence. Mid-level administrators occupy something like a middle stratum: consequential, under-acknowledged at home.
- The **managerial revolution** has expanded administrative staffing and professionalised it, changing where decisions are actually made.

**What this predicts, practically:**
- There is often no single person who can answer a cross-cutting question, and no contradiction is implied when two offices give inconsistent answers. **Get the answer from the office that owns the decision** — the registrar for credits, the international office for visa status, the bar authority for eligibility, financial aid for aid.
- A commitment from a faculty member is not a commitment from the institution, and vice versa.
- Slowness is usually structural, not personal, and should be planned around rather than argued with.

## 6. Stratification and institutional type

American higher education is not a system but a landscape of institutional types with different missions, funding models, and constraints: research universities, comprehensive and regional institutions, liberal arts colleges, community colleges, minority-serving institutions, and for-profits — the last of which Thelin insists on including rather than treating as an aberration.

- **Carnegie classification** is the standard descriptive framework and is about institutional type and activity, not quality — though it is routinely misread as a ranking.
- Membership signals such as the **Association of American Universities** mark research standing.
- Prestige is unusually **sticky**: it accrues slowly, is defended vigorously, and correlates with resources more than with instructional quality.

**Reading rule:** identify the institution's type and funding position before interpreting any of its behaviour. A regional public under budget pressure, an elite private with a large endowment, and a for-profit have genuinely different objective functions, and the same action means different things at each.

## 7. Research, philanthropy, and internationalisation

- **Research and development** operates on external funding with its own compliance apparatus, and indirect-cost recovery is a real institutional interest. Research capacity is not evenly distributed and is not the same thing as teaching quality.
- **Fund-raising and philanthropy** are a distinct revenue stream with a distinct logic; donor priorities shape what gets built and funded, and named gifts are visible evidence of where institutional attention has gone.
- **Internationalisation** is treated as one of the major contemporary developments — and it is driven by a mixture of genuine academic mission and revenue strategy. **Both motives are usually present simultaneously**, and a student is better served by assuming both than by assuming either alone.

## Decision rules

- **Never compare schools on sticker price.** Compare net price, and treat it as unknown until an aid offer exists.
- **Ask what the unit's budget structure is.** Under RCM, enrollment is revenue for that unit, and recruitment intensity should be discounted accordingly.
- **Institutions absorb new revenue** (Bowen). Expect price to track prestige ambition, not cost.
- **Aspiring institutions spend hardest.** Discretionary merit money is most available where a school is climbing, not where it has arrived.
- **Read the budget, not the mission statement**, for what a school values.
- **Route questions to the office that owns the decision.** Inconsistent answers across offices are normal and not evidence of bad faith.
- **Classify the institution before interpreting its behaviour** — type, funding position, and prestige trajectory.
- **Assume mission and revenue motives coexist** in international recruitment; act on the revenue one when assessing claims, on the mission one when assessing the education.
