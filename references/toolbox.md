# references/toolbox.md

**Sources:** Heuer & Pherson, *Structured Analytic Techniques for Intelligence Analysis* (2011); ACH originates in Heuer, *Psychology of Intelligence Analysis* (1999), ch. 8.
**Trigger:** load when a decision is **consequential enough to earn the extra step**. The core carries only the underlying habit (generate alternatives, seek to disprove, report rejects); the full procedures live here and are invoked deliberately, not run on every question.
**What this file is:** callable procedures. No temperament language — the disposition to reach for these lives in the core.

## When is a decision "consequential enough"?

Reach for a full technique when **any** of these hold; otherwise the core habit is sufficient:
- **Irreversible or costly to unwind** — accepting an offer, declining bar eligibility, a visa-affecting choice.
- **Contested** — credible reasoning points in more than one direction, or the user and the advisor disagree.
- **Arrived too easily** — the leading answer showed up early and fits expectations suspiciously well (a flag, not a virtue).
- **High-stakes on the user's own weighting** — it sits at the top of their stated priorities.
Match the technique to the need; do not tax a routine question with heavy machinery.

## Selection guide — which technique for which need

| The need | Reach for |
|---|---|
| Too few options on the table | Structured Brainstorming; Starbursting; Multiple Hypothesis Generation |
| Several viable answers, must choose | Analysis of Competing Hypotheses |
| Comparing schools/offers on many criteria | Decision Matrix |
| One option is leading and it arrived easily | Key Assumptions Check; Devil's Advocacy; Premortem |
| Evidence base may be thin or echoing | Quality of Information Check |
| A plan needs strengthening, not judging | Pros-Cons-Faults-and-Fixes |
| A rare but ruinous outcome (visa denial, funding collapse) | High Impact/Low Probability Analysis |
| The conclusion depends on the future not surprising us | Indicators; What If? Analysis |
| Reading the gatekeeper's behaviour | Red Hat Analysis |
| Missing the context outside the immediate question | Outside-In Thinking |
| Assessing an agent, consultant, or program's claims | Deception-aware source review (§ below) |

Heuer & Pherson designate a **core set** every analyst should know: Structured Brainstorming, Cross-Impact Matrix, Key Assumptions Check, Indicators and the Indicator Validator™, ACH, Premortem Analysis, and Structured Self-Critique. Everything else is situational.

## Analysis of Competing Hypotheses (ACH) — full 8-step procedure

Use for a contested, consequential judgment with several viable explanations or options. The governing principle: **seek the hypothesis with the least *inconsistent* evidence, not the most consistent** — refutation discriminates, confirmation does not.

1. **Enumerate hypotheses** — generate a full set of plausible alternatives *before* filtering for plausibility. Bring in other views to avoid a too-short list. Include options that cannot yet be ruled out.
2. **List evidence and arguments** — the relevant facts, and the load-bearing assumptions, for and against each. Include absence of evidence where a hypothesis predicts something that is not observed.
3. **Build the matrix** — hypotheses across the top, evidence down the side. For each cell, mark whether the item is **Consistent (C)**, **Inconsistent (I)**, or **Not Applicable (N/A)** with that hypothesis. Work across each row (one piece of evidence against all hypotheses), not down each column.
4. **Refine the matrix** — delete evidence and arguments with no diagnostic value (items consistent with every hypothesis discriminate nothing, however true). Restate or split hypotheses as needed.
5. **Draw tentative conclusions by seeking to disprove** — rank hypotheses by weight of **inconsistency**, not consistency. The hypothesis with the fewest inconsistencies is provisionally strongest; the one with the most is provisionally rejected.
6. **Analyze sensitivity** — identify the few items of evidence driving the conclusion. Ask how the conclusion changes if a key item is wrong, misleading, or subject to deception. Note which linchpin items would most change the answer.
7. **Report conclusions** — give the relative likelihood of *all* hypotheses, not just the most likely one, and state the rejected alternatives with the reasons they were rejected.
8. **Identify milestones / indicators** — specify future observable events that would be inconsistent with the current conclusion and would signal it needs revisiting. Hand these to the user as a watch-list.

## Key Assumptions Check

Trigger: before committing to a consequential judgment, or when a conclusion feels solid without obvious reason.
1. List the assumptions the current line of reasoning rests on (the things being taken for granted).
2. For each, ask: why do we believe this? What would happen to the judgment if it were wrong?
3. Classify each as solidly supported, correct-with-caveats, or unsupported/questionable.
4. Flag any *linchpin* assumption — one that, if false, collapses the conclusion. Refine or re-source it before proceeding.

## Quality of Information Check

Trigger: before relying on a body of evidence for a consequential call.
- Review the sources for accuracy, and for whether they are genuinely **independent** of one another (multiple accounts tracing to one origin are one source).
- Distinguish what is verified fact from what is assumed or inferred within the evidence itself.
- Note gaps, dated items, and anything a source had reason to shade. Mark single-sourced claims as provisional.

**Deception-aware extension** — apply when a source has a financial interest in the answer (recruiters, paid admission consultants, educational agents, program marketing, ranking-driven self-reports):
- Ask what the source is compensated for, and whether the compensation depends on the user's decision going one way.
- Ask whether an unfavourable fact would ever reach the user through this channel. A source that structurally cannot report bad news is not corroboration.
- Separate the source's **verifiable claims** (which can be checked against an official register) from its **unverifiable claims** (placement rates, "we have great relationships"). Route the second kind to provisional.

## Indicators / Indicators Validator™

Trigger: whenever a strategy or forecast is issued (pairs with ACH step 8).
- Define concrete, observable indicators that would show a scenario developing or a strategy's key assumption failing.
- Keep only **diagnostic** indicators: one that is equally consistent with every outcome is useless. A good indicator is consistent with the outcome it tracks and *inconsistent* with the alternatives.
- Tie each indicator to the revision it should trigger: *if [indicator], then [what changes]*.

## Hypothesis and option generation

Use when the list of options is suspiciously short — the most common upstream failure, since no downstream technique can rescue a set that omits the right answer.

- **Structured Brainstorming** — a divergent pass with no evaluation permitted, then a convergent pass that groups and prunes. Silent written generation before discussion when one voice would otherwise dominate (Nominal Group Technique).
- **Starbursting** — generate *questions* rather than answers, systematically across who / what / when / where / why / how. Useful at intake, when the real task is finding out what has not been asked.
- **Multiple Hypothesis Generation** — build the option set combinatorially (e.g., program type × specialization × jurisdiction) rather than by recall, so that unglamorous but viable combinations survive to the matrix.
- **Cross-Impact Matrix** — list the variables or actors, then examine each *pair* and state how one affects the other. Surfaces interactions that a linear list hides (e.g., how a funding decision constrains a visa category, which constrains a work option).

## Decision Matrix

Trigger: choosing among several concrete options against multiple criteria — the standard shape of a school or offer comparison.
1. List the options as columns and the criteria as rows.
2. **Weight the criteria before scoring any option.** Weighting after the scores are visible is rationalization.
3. Score each option against each criterion.
4. Compute, then — critically — **inspect the result rather than obeying it.** The matrix's function is to expose which criterion is actually driving the choice and how thin the margin is, not to make the decision.
5. Run sensitivity: which single weight or score, if changed, flips the ranking? That is the fact to verify and to watch.

Pair with the core's ranking discipline: an option that is disqualified on a hard constraint does not get rescued by a high total.

## Pros-Cons-Faults-and-Fixes

Trigger: an option is leading and the task is to strengthen or break it, not to re-rank the field.
1. List pros and cons.
2. **Faults** — challenge each pro: under what conditions does it fail to hold?
3. **Fixes** — for each con, ask whether a modification neutralizes it, or whether it is structural.
An option whose cons are all fixable and whose pros are all fault-prone is weaker than its list length suggests.

## High Impact/Low Probability Analysis

Trigger: an outcome that is unlikely but would be ruinous and is being ignored *because* it is unlikely — visa refusal, funding withdrawal, program closure, a credential not recognized.
1. Name the outcome plainly and assume it happens.
2. Identify the plausible pathways that would produce it.
3. Specify the observable early indicators for each pathway.
4. Identify what mitigation is available now and what its cost is.
The output is not a probability. It is a contingency and a watch-list, which is what the user needs regardless of the probability.

## What If? Analysis

Trigger: a conclusion is stable and confidence is high. Assert that the surprising event **has already happened**, then reason backward to how — the reframing that a straight probability estimate suppresses.

## Challenge techniques (Heuer & Pherson, ch. 9)

Reserve for high-stakes or contested conclusions. Their common purpose is to break an established mental model, since postmortems of major analytic failures repeatedly identify an unquestioned mindset as the driver.
- **Devil's Advocacy** — deliberately build the strongest case *against* the leading conclusion.
- **"What would have to be true?"** — for the leading option, state the conditions that must hold for it to be correct, then test whether they do.
- **Red Hat Analysis** — model the decision from the other party's point of view (here: the admissions committee, examiner, or employer) using *their* incentives and constraints, not your own.
- **Premortem** — assume the chosen course has failed; work backward to the plausible causes; address the reachable ones now.
- **Structured Self-Critique** — a team or checklist pass that interrogates the group's own conclusion for the assumptions and evidence gaps it is motivated to overlook.
- **Outside-In Thinking** — before analyzing the specific question, list the external forces that could act on it (regulatory, economic, institutional, political) and ask which reach this case. Counters the tendency to reason entirely inside the frame the user brought.

## Reporting the output

Whatever the technique, the deliverable back to the user follows the core's rules: the ranked options with the rejected ones named and the reason for rejection, the linchpin assumption identified, the sensitivity fact stated, and the watch-list handed over. **A technique that produces a conclusion but not its trip-wires has not been finished.**
